In times when digital archives are, on one side, weaponized through surveillance, misinformation, and erasure to serve manufactured narratives driven by state and economic interests, advancing digital technologies and grassroots movements have, on the other side, enabled archiving to become increasingly decentralized and reclaimed by civil society, activists, and artist communities. Archiving is no longer the exclusive domain of institutions; it has expanded through collective and open-source practices rooted in knowledge sharing, accountability, and forensic methodologies to disrupt and reframe the politics of documentation. 

The Non-Archive case study explores this expanded landscape through experimental publishing. Facilitated by the digital department of TBA21–Academy, the project centers on the potential of open access and open source principles as a practice of making public while remaining accountable to the contexts in which information emerges. It draws on TBA21's Ocean-Archive.org (OA), a digital platform, storytelling medium, and pedagogical tool that began as an archival initiative, a database that has since evolved through community building, storytelling, and programming. Taking elements of the Ocean Archives structure with the question "How would you like to dive in?" as an invitation and conceptual starting point, the case study proposal expands on modes of experience that foreground subjectivity, situated knowledge, and plurality. 

Countering the shortcomings of institutionalized archives that attempt to separate subversive movements by isolating events/people, our group is guided by building a decentralized, open-source model that locates the present with border histories and struggles. With a common understanding of non-archive not just as a passive space for reflection of the past but instead as a tool for active engagement and resistance in the present.

# MODES OF EXPERIENCE 

From the beginning, we rejected the idea of objectivity or neutrality. Instead, the proposal plays intentionally with subjectivity and its mediated nature. The informational space is entered from the perspective of the characters. Each character corresponds to a conceptual "shelf" within the archive, which reflects a situated perspective grounded in the diverse backgrounds and research practices of the team behind Non-Archives. 

Rather than homogenizing these differences into a singular voice, we let them co-exist and interfere. Each character functions as a mediating perspective, carrying the partial truths, cultural sensibilities, and disciplinary biases of the mode of experience.

The Linear Mode traces the inputs, encounters, and methodologies that shaped the proposal. The Files Mode gives direct access to the raw documentation and research materials that underpinned the work. The Infrastructure Mode exposes the archival system's backend—revealing where the archive is hosted, how it is served, and the dependencies that shape its form and accessibility. The Spatial Mode introduces a navigable 3D environment built using the open-source Godot game engine. Within this environment, archival content is geographically and politically re-situated—mapped back into the terrain from which it emerged, transforming the archive from a static repository into a spatial tool for memory, advocacy, and resistance.

Within the Non-Archive platform, the content is activated through shifting between these characters, with the intent to support the embodied navigation of the archive from different standpoints. Together, these modes offer a decentralized, non-extractive approach to archiving: democratic and accessible, future-oriented rather than past-bound, situated within specific political geographies, and solidaristic in its refusal to flatten difference. The archive becomes not a fixed container but a living interface, which opens itself to co-authorship, reconfiguration, and collective struggle.

# DIGITAL MEMORY

Navigating through a digital landscape shaped by data extraction and enclosure, the case study was paired with the OSP collective (Open Source Publishing) as pulsos to dive into open-source infrastructures and critically reflect on the technologies that underpin digital memory. Together, we interrogated the tools, protocols, and systems we often take for granted in our daily digital interactions: file systems, interfaces, and hosting models. Through workshops and input sessions, we traced the narratives embedded in metadata, navigated infrastructures behind firewalls by using Terminal, coded in HTML, and connected to a self-hosted server. These open-access approaches started to inform not only our methodology but also opened space to question how digital memory is stored, accessed, and shared under the conditions of platform capitalism and control. 

Digital infrastructures are often described as invisible background processes that "just work." However, this invisibility is itself ideological. Digital infrastructures are neither immaterial nor neutral. They are built on extractive economies, labor inequalities, and specific cultural assumptions. They exist in servers housed in physical space, cooled by real water, powered by electrical grids, and maintained by workers. They are written not just in code but also in policy, geography, and intention.

To make these material conditions tangible, the project's website hosts an Infrastructure mode of experience. This layer reveals the physical locations of both the hosting server and the user accessing related to the project, foregrounding the spatial and political realities of digital exchange. Where are you? Where is the non-archive data? Where is the Ocean Archive data? Where is the Organismo data?

# ON THE GROUND

If the infrastructures behind archives are rooted in space, can the digital non-archive itself be returned to place? This question opened up a line of conversations and investigations that unfolded throughout our research:  How to re-situate technology within its social, ecological, and material realities? What would it take to reembedding the digital in its spatial contexts? 

During our case study trip, we began experimenting with this idea through an app interface that allows for location-based archival interaction based on walks and encounters we had. By zooming into a map of Gascueña, you encounter images and sound files embedded in coordinates of the landscape, which can be traced in the Map mode of experience. 

The context of Gascueña in the region of Cuenca, which, alongside other central parts of Spain, is often referred to as La España vacía ("empty Spain") or La España vacate ("emptied Spain"), has been an interesting testing ground to counter this notion of "empty "terrain. Not from the intention to fill this so-called emptiness but to make its density visible. What appears as the absence is, in fact, dense with memory, labor, and entangled histories of infrastructural neglect, migration, and uneven development. Alongside our local guide, Santiago Jarabo, we traced the ancient Greek lapis specularis mining path, visited the former wine caves hidden beneath the houses of the village, and had conversations around along the way. Paying attention to these spatially embedded stories highlights that memory bleeds into the material and political realities of place. They are lived, contested, and carried by the land and people themselves.

Guided by this situated understanding, we reached out to the local initiative "Gascueña y Tinajas contra las macrogranjas ." As part of the broader platform Pueblos Vivos, the more than 20 neighborhood movements from different towns in the province of Cuenca come together under the shared fight to defend their land and water resources from the expansion of industrial livestock operations. Their approach of gathering, organizing, and activating spatial data of tendered plots to build up their legal case against the commodification of lands, disruption of small-scale industries, and the pollution of the local freshwater resources is not only a form of grassroots resistance but also a situated archival act as a productive further step connecting their hyperlocalized struggle to broader struggles, tracing a line from rural Cuenca to global systems of extractive agriculture, water injustice, and land dispossession.

# CONNECTIONS

A key element in articulating this relational logic was the development tool that maps shared struggles and contributes to shaping collective knowledge. Using the Ocean Archive API; it visualizes the archive as a network of tags, keywords, and authors. These elements appear as nodes, connected by lines that represent thematic, political, or conceptual links. For example, a tag like "water justice" might connect a legal case, an artistic intervention, and a scientific report—tracing solidarities across disciplines and geographies. This mode of indexing enables a way to see relationships between disparate contexts, trace affinities between frontline communities, and foster a language of struggle across geographies. 

Zooming in to a local case study and then zooming out to view its entanglement within broader movements, the archive becomes a tool for shaping collective knowledge and mapping shared concerns across scales. Each contribution remaps the whole, shifting the structure from accumulation to relation.

# SPATIAL ARCHIVE 

To further investigate how archives can serve as tools for advocacy in their spatial context and connect local and broader struggles, we invited a guest lecture from Earshot, an NGO focused on sonic investigation, documentation, and advocacy. Their open-source methodologies document the auditory experiences and harm enacted on the Jawlani community in the name of green and clean energy. Layered within the digital interactive format of Zifzafa, it was specifically interesting to the case study how these digital archives go beyond mere documentation and function as a repository for advocacy. Using real spatial data and sound modeling, Zifzafa demonstrates how digital environments can expose the otherwise invisible. Turning spatial simulation into a political and evidentiary tool to advocate and preserve the auditory life of the Jawlan and aims to contest further acts of occupation and dispossession by the Israeli state.

This approach resonated deeply with our intention to re-situate the archive in place—not as a metaphor, but as a method. Inspired by their use of open-source game development as a form of spatial storytelling and accountability, we built our project using the Godot game engine, an open-source platform that allowed us to construct a navigable 3D environment tailored to our conceptual framework. Godot's lightweight architecture and accessibility are aligned with our commitment to transparency, adaptability, and collective ownership.

In our proposal, the digital terrain is generated from heightmaps derived from a Mapbox add-on, creating a topographically accurate digital twin of the Gascueña landscape. This virtual geography functions not as a backdrop but as an interface: a living archive in which content—images, audio recordings, testimonies, documents—is embedded directly into the coordinates of specific locations.

By reattaching archival material to terrain, the project resists memory abstraction into folders and metadata. Instead, it offers a form of situated navigation where users move through digital geographies that echo real landscapes. Memory becomes spatial, embodied, and context-aware—emerging through interaction with the terrain itself.

# PUBLISHING & APPLICATION SCENARIOS

The website, as our primary publishing platform, is designed around modes of experience, each offering a different level of technical complexity. This layered structure acknowledges that users arrive with diverse capacities, devices, and infrastructures. Rather than enforcing a single way of engaging with the material, it accommodates multiple entry points.
